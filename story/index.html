Srinagar, Kashmir

Chapter 1 of 4

The Valley

Kashmir sits between high mountain ranges, where winter arrives early and water shapes daily life. Srinagar grew around the Jhelum River and Dal Lake, close to trading routes that connected the valley with Central Asia, the high plateau, and the plains of India.

The city became a meeting place for many exacting arts. Papier-mâché painters build colour in fine layers. Carpet makers knot complex fields from memory and pattern maps. Walnut carvers read grain before cutting. Shawl makers brought the same patience to fibre, thread, loom, and needle.

Pashmina begins farther east, where Changthangi goats grow a soft winter undercoat against the cold. The raw fibre reaches Kashmir before specialists clean it, spin it on the yinder, dye the yarn, weave the cloth, and finish its surface by hand.

Chapter 2 of 4

A Family Collection

Woven Hymns is a family-led collection shaped by Kashmir and by the people who know this work closely. The aim is simple: present each shawl with enough context that its material, technique, and time can be understood before anyone asks about price.

That means looking beyond a finished surface. We want to know who handled the fibre, which technique formed the pattern, how long the work occupied a loom or embroidery frame, and what makes one piece different from the next. A shawl deserves more than a luxury label. It deserves a clear account of how it came to be.

The collection is also a reason for the family to gather its own record. Names, dated photographs, workshop memories, and the details remembered by elders will be added only after they have been checked together. What is shared publicly should be specific enough to be useful and honest enough to last.

Chapter 3 of 4

Many Hands

One shawl rarely belongs to a single pair of hands. A sorter separates coarse guard hair from fine down. A spinner turns loose fibre into an even thread. A dyer matches colour by eye. A weaver controls tension across the width of the loom. Some pieces then move to an embroiderer for months of Sozni needlework.

These are separate professions. Each has its own vocabulary, tools, and judgement. A skilled weaver does not automatically become a skilled embroiderer, and a dyer can spend a working life learning how water, season, fibre, and drying change a shade.

Knowledge travels through observation and repetition. An apprentice begins with yarn, pattern names, and small corrections before taking responsibility for a full piece. The work continues because experienced makers have the time, trust, and paid commissions needed to teach someone else.

Chapter 4 of 4

What We Carry Forward

To carry this work forward is not to freeze it in the past. It is to protect the conditions that make careful work possible: sound fibre, patient hands, fair time, and a buyer who understands why speed changes the result.

We look for pieces that reward close attention. The edge should feel considered. Pattern should hold together at a distance and reveal small decisions up close. The reverse, the joins, and the finish should make sense when the shawl is turned in the hand.

This site is our way of slowing the first encounter down. Begin with the making process, then look at the collection. If a piece stays with you, the next step is a direct conversation with the family, not a countdown or a checkout screen.
