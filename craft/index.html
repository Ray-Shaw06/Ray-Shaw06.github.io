From Srinagar, Kashmir

Eight stages. Many specialists. One finished shawl.

Pashmina does not move from raw fibre to finished cloth in one room. It passes through a chain of trained hands, each responsible for a different decision.

Part one

The journey of a shawl

Durations are working ranges. Technique, pattern density, weather, and the condition of the fibre can all change the calendar.

  1. 01

    Each spring

    Collecting the fibre

    On the high plateau, Changthangi goats grow a soft undercoat beneath their coarse outer hair. When the animals begin to shed in spring, herding families comb the loose fibre rather than shearing the whole fleece. The two layers remain mixed at this point. A single goat may yield only about 100 to 150 grams of usable fine down after cleaning, so the material for one shawl begins with fibre gathered from several animals.

    Specialist
    Changpa herding families
    Method
    Seasonal hand combing
    Result
    Raw pashm
  2. 02

    About 1 to 2 weeks

    Cleaning & sorting

    The raw pashm arrives tangled with coarse guard hair, dust, and vegetable matter. Sorters open small amounts at a time and remove anything that would make the yarn harsh or uneven. They judge softness, length, and colour with their fingers, repeating the work until the remaining down is light and consistent. The loss in weight is considerable, but this careful separation is what allows the cleaned fibre to be spun into an exceptionally fine thread.

    Specialist
    Fibre sorters
    Method
    Opening, picking, and dehairing by hand
    Result
    Clean fine down
  3. 03

    About 3 to 4 weeks

    Hand spinning

    The cleaned fibre is spun on a yinder, a small traditional wheel turned by hand. The spinner draws out a narrow ribbon of down and controls the twist by touch. Too much twist can make the yarn hard. Too little can make it weak. A steady thread depends on thousands of small adjustments that are difficult to measure but easy for an experienced hand to feel. The finished yarn is wound carefully so that it can move to dyeing or directly to the loom.

    Specialist
    Specialist spinners
    Method
    Yinder hand wheel
    Result
    Fine continuous yarn
  4. 04

    Several days

    Dyeing

    Yarn is dyed in small batches before weaving or embroidery. The dyer judges colour while the fibre is wet, then anticipates how it will change as it dries. Water, temperature, fibre quality, and the size of a batch all affect the result. When several shades must meet inside one Kani pattern, consistency matters across every skein. The dyed yarn is rinsed, dried, compared with the intended palette, and sorted for the next specialist.

    Specialist
    Small-batch dyers
    Method
    Colour matched by eye
    Result
    Prepared warp and weft yarn
  5. 05

    Several weeks to many months

    Weaving

    The yarn is arranged as warp and weft on a wooden handloom. For a plain pashmina, the weaver maintains an even surface and clean edge across the full width. A Kani shawl works differently. The design is read from a coded pattern, and colour is carried through the weave with many small wooden bobbins called kanis. Every change of colour must be placed while the cloth is being formed. A complex field can advance by only a small amount in one day and may occupy a loom for months or years.

    Specialist
    Handloom weavers
    Method
    Wooden loom, shuttle, or Kani bobbins
    Result
    Finished woven cloth
  6. 06

    1 to 6 months or longer

    Embroidery

    When a shawl calls for Sozni, the woven cloth moves to an embroiderer. A fine needle places silk, cotton, or wool thread into the surface without making the fabric stiff. Borders, boteh forms, leaves, and flowers grow through repeated short stitches. The embroiderer must preserve spacing, direction, and tension across a large area, often for months. On the finest work, the reverse is clean enough to echo the pattern on the face, which lets a careful buyer read the control of the needle from both sides.

    Specialist
    Sozni embroiderers
    Method
    Fine single-needle work
    Result
    A patterned surface
  7. 07

    About 1 week

    Washing & finishing

    The completed cloth is washed so the weave can settle and the natural hand of the fibre can emerge. It is dried carefully rather than forced through an industrial finishing line. A purzgar then studies the surface and removes stray fibres or small irregularities with hand tools. This is not a cosmetic shortcut. The specialist must clean the face without thinning the cloth, pulling a thread, or flattening the character created by hand spinning and weaving.

    Specialist
    Washers and purzgars
    Method
    Cold washing, drying, and hand clipping
    Result
    A settled weave and clean surface
  8. 08

    Several days

    Final inspection

    The shawl is opened fully, held against the light, and examined from edge to edge. The checker looks for weak threads, uneven joins, missed stitches, colour changes, and repairs that need further attention. Dimensions and finish are checked again before the piece is folded. This final reading brings every earlier decision back into view. Fibre, spin, tension, pattern, needlework, washing, and clipping must now work as one cloth.

    Specialist
    The final checker
    Method
    Backlight and hand inspection
    Result
    An approved finished shawl

A plain shawl can take months. Detailed Kani weaving or Sozni embroidery can keep a piece in skilled hands for more than a year.

Part two

Four names, four different things to notice

Some names describe the fibre. Others describe how pattern enters the cloth. Looking at technique makes the collection easier to understand.

The pattern is woven into the cloth with small wooden bobbins.

Kani

Kani patterns are formed during weaving, not added after the cloth leaves the loom. The weaver follows a coded design and carries many colours across the warp with small wooden bobbins called kanis. Each curve, leaf, and flower is built one thread at a time. Look closely at the reverse and the pattern still belongs to the structure of the cloth. A complex Kani can occupy a loom for many months or several years.

Fine needle embroidery worked over woven pashmina.

Sozni

Sozni begins after the pashmina cloth has been woven. An embroiderer uses silk, cotton, or fine wool thread to build botanical forms, borders, and boteh motifs with a single needle. Months of controlled stitching can fill a shawl without taking away its softness. The reverse often reveals the quality of the work: short, clean stitches and a pattern that closely follows the face show how precisely the thread was handled.

Pattern covers most or all of the shawl's field.

Jamawar

Jamawar describes a richly patterned shawl whose design covers most or all of the surface. Historic examples were prized in Mughal courts and later collected in Europe. The density of the field demands long, patient work and careful colour control. On woven Jamawar, motifs are placed as the cloth is formed. Some later pieces combine woven structure with complementary handwork, so the individual construction should always be examined rather than assumed from the name alone.

Fine winter underfleece from the Changthangi goat.

Pashmina

Pashmina names the fine winter underfleece of the Changthangi goat, raised on the high plateau beyond Kashmir. It is the material foundation beneath plain, Kani, Sozni, and Jamawar shawls. Good pashmina feels warm without feeling heavy and can be spun into an unusually fine yarn. The finished quality still depends on what happens next: sorting, spinning, dyeing, weaving, washing, and the judgement used at every stage.

How to read what you see

A close view, the reverse, and the edge can tell you more than a label. These four checks are a useful place to begin.

Pattern in the weave
On Kani and woven Jamawar, colour enters while the cloth is being formed. The design belongs to the structure, not only to the surface.
Needlework over cloth
On Sozni, the pashmina is woven first. An embroiderer then builds the pattern with a fine needle and separate thread.
The reverse side
Turn the shawl over. Clean joins, controlled stitches, and a readable reverse often reveal more about the maker's precision than the face alone.
The edge and finish
Look along the full edge and hold the cloth against light. Even tension, a clean surface, and small human variations are signs worth reading together.

What the time is paying for

Fine fibre matters, but the finished quality comes from a sequence of trained judgements. Time is spent sorting, controlling tension, placing colour, correcting small faults, and finishing the cloth without flattening its character.
